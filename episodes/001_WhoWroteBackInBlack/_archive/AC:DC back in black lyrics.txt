
Ladies and gentlemen of the jury.

Before I present a single piece of evidence, I want to say something clearly.

This case is not an attack on anyone. It is not a diminishment of anyone's talent or courage or grief. What happened in February 1980 was a tragedy. And what followed was one of the most remarkable examples of courage, creativity, and master-craft, in the history of rock and roll.

I want to begin there. With the truth that everyone agrees on.

Ronald Belford Scott — Bon Scott — was one of a kind.

Born in Scotland. Raised in Australia. In trouble early, in bands early, living at full velocity from the moment he understood what velocity was. He was not merely a rock singer. He was a force of nature who had found the perfect vehicle for everything he was.

He joined AC/DC in 1974. He took a band of scrappy young Australians and gave them something no amount of musical talent could manufacture — a personality. A voice. Not just the voice you heard on the records, that extraordinary ragged growl, but a literary voice. A point of view. A narrator who was reckless and funny and dangerous and absolutely, unmistakably alive.

Six albums. 1979's Highway to Hell their breakthrough. The world finally paying attention. The momentum building toward something enormous.

And then on the 19th of February, 1980, Bon Scott died.

He was 33 years old.

He was found in a car in London. Acute alcohol poisoning. He had been out the night before, and he never woke up.

The music world was stunned. AC/DC was finished. Everyone said so. You do not lose a voice like that and continue. You cannot replace what cannot be replaced.

The band leaders, Malcolm and Angus Young disagreed.

They grieved. They considered stopping, but then they made a decision that took extraordinary courage — they would continue. They would find a new singer. They would make the next record. Not despite Bon Scott, but FOR him.

They found Brian Johnson. A journeyman rock singer from Newcastle, England. Modest success with a band called Geordie through the 1970s. He was not famous. He was not an obvious choice. But he walked into that audition and sang with everything he had, and something passed between the Young brothers, and they knew that they had their man.

What followed was, by any measure, extraordinary.

Within weeks — not months, within weeks — they were recording. Brian Johnson, brand new to the band, alongside grieving men he had barely met, stepped into an impossible situation with the eyes of the rock world upon him.

And he delivered. Oh did he ever deliver... 

Back in Black. Released July 1980. Fifty million copies sold. The second best-selling album in the history of recorded music. A monument. An event. One of those rare cultural moments that permanently rearranges the landscape.

The story the world tells is this: a band shattered by tragedy found a hero. Brian Johnson stepped up when everything was on the line. He wrote lyrics under pressure. He sang those words he had written with jaw dropping, ferocious energy. He carried the band through its darkest hour and out the other side into the greatest triumph of their career.

It is a beautiful story.

It is a story of resilience and courage and the refusal to be broken.

I am standing here today because that story — that beautiful, heroic story — has a problem.

The Problem?

The problem is the lyrics.

Not the music. The music is unambiguously the Young brothers. Their riffs. Their arrangements. Their electrifying structural intelligence. Nobody disputes that.

The problem is the words.

Because when you examine those words carefully — when you look at them the way a literary historian examines a disputed manuscript, or the way a forensic scientist examines a crime scene — something becomes very difficult to explain.

Those words do not read like Brian Johnson.

They read like Bon Scott.

And I am going to prove it to you. Step by step. Exhibit by exhibit. Until the conclusion is not a theory, not a suspicion, but an inevitability.

Exhibit One. The Fingerprint.

Members of the jury, before I show you a single lyric, I need to establish something fundamental. Something that will be the lens through which you examine everything that follows.

In forensic science we rely on fingerprints because they cannot be faked. They are unique to each individual. They are left unconsciously. They are present whether the person intended to leave them or not. And they do not lie.

Literature has fingerprints too.

Not favourite words. Not favourite subjects. Those can be imitated by anyone paying sufficient attention. A literary fingerprint is something far deeper and far harder to replicate — it is a writer's sensibility. The personality behind the words. The particular way they see the world and cannot help but show it, regardless of the technical form they are working in. It bleeds through whether they are writing about themselves, about a woman, or telling someone else's story entirely.

You cannot contain it. You cannot switch it off. It is as involuntary as a heartbeat.

Bon Scott's fingerprint had four components. And they always travelled together.

The first was the narrator. A specific narrator, inhabited presence behind every song. A man. With a past, with appetites, with a reputation that preceded him into every room. 

The second is the storytelling. The story structure that is the foundation of each song. You always knew whose eyes you were seeing that story through, and that he was taking you somewhere. He could make you laugh and make you lean forward at exactly the same moment. That is not accidental. That is craft. 

The third was the wit. The mischief, the wink. A delight in transgression. A twinkle behind the danger. He wrote about excess and desire and bad behaviour Even at his most outrageous there was an impishness — the grin of someone who knows exactly how far he is pushing it and is enjoying every single second. Behind every lyric was an intelligent man making a precise and deliberate choice.

And the Third. Perhaps the most important of all.

The swagger.

The swagger of a man who has actually lived the life he is singing about. Who knows it. And who cannot quite believe his luck. Bon Scott's songs always strut like a rooster, it's unmistakable. 

These four qualities — the narrator, the storytelling, the wit, and the swagger — are the fingerprint. Inseparable. Traveling together always. Present in everything Bon Scott ever wrote.

Remember them.

Exhibit Two. The Fingerprint on Confirmed Bon Scott Compositions.

Now let me show you the fingerprint in action.

These are lyrics written and recorded between 1974 and 1979. Songs beyond any dispute. Songs the whole world agrees belong to Bon Scott.

See me ride out of the sunset on your colour TV screen. Out for all that I can get if you know what I mean.

The narrator. Present. Specific. Announcing himself. And already — if you know what I mean — the wit. The wink. The suggestion that there is another layer operating just beneath the surface. In the very first lines.

I'm gonna be a rock and roll singer.

The swagger. Unadorned. A young man from Perth, Australia, telling the world exactly what he intends to be. Not hoping. Not trying. Gonna be. Already certain.

I'm on a highway to hell.

The mischief. He is describing his own life — the touring, the drinking, the excess — and he is doing it with a grin so wide you can hear it. A less intelligent writer would have made this dark. Bon Scott made it a celebration.

I've got big balls.

Members of the jury — that is a man spending three and a half minutes discussing the grandeur of high society balls. Crystal chandeliers. Distinguished guests. The finest social occasions. And simultaneously — simultaneously — discussing something else entirely. And making you smile every time the chorus lands because you cannot quite believe he is getting away with it.

That is not vulgarity. That is genius. That is a man operating on two levels at once with complete control of both.
And now — I want to linger on one song in particular. Because it is important. Not just as evidence of the fingerprint — but because we will return to it at a critical moment in this case.

Touch Too Much.

I was so satisfied, deep down inside, like a hand in a velvet glove.

Close your eyes and hear that line. The passion of it on the surface. The romance. And underneath — something else entirely. Something that arrives a half second after the words land and produces, in any attentive listener, a slow smile of recognition.

That is Bon Scott at the height of his powers. All four elements present simultaneously. The intimate narrator. The wit working on two levels. The mischief dancing behind every word. And the swagger of a man who knows precisely what he has just done and is already moving on to the next line.

Hold that feeling. We will need it.

Exhibit Three. The Control Sample. Brian Johnson, Before.

Every fingerprint case requires a control sample. A known reference point against which we measure what we find at the scene.

We have one.

Brian Johnson was not without a catalogue before AC/DC. His band Geordie released records throughout the 1970s. We know exactly how this man wrote when he was entirely, unambiguously himself.

Look for the fingerprint.

It is not there.

What Johnson wrote for Geordie is competent. Professional. It does what rock lyrics are supposed to do. But the narrator is generic. The wit is absent. The double meaning, the layered mischief, the particular inhabited swagger of a man reporting from the frontline of his own specific life — nowhere to be found.
He came from the world of glam rock. Geordie were trying to sound like Slade and Sweet. That is a world of performance and spectacle — of dressing up, of putting on a show. It is almost the philosophical opposite of what Bon Scott did. Bon Scott did not dress up. Bon Scott reported.

Brian Johnson wrote like a rock singer. A very good rock singer.

Bon Scott wrote like a man who happened to be in a rock band.

These are not the same thing. And the control sample proves it beyond any reasonable question.

The fingerprint is absent. Completely. Before Back in Black.

Remember that.

Exhibit Four. The Timeline.

I want to pause and ask you to consider something that should, on its own, give every one of you significant pause.
Bon Scott died on the 19th of February 1980.

Brian Johnson was hired within weeks.

Recording began almost immediately.

And the result was the greatest rock album ever made.

Ladies and gentlemen — the greatest rock album ever made.

I want you to sit with the implications of that timeline.

We are asked to believe that a man who had never written songs with Scott's point of view, storytelling, wit and swagger — whose entire previous catalogue demonstrates a fundamentally different literary sensibility — walked into a studio weeks after joining a band he barely knew, while the wound of Bon Scott's death was still raw and open, and produced not just competent lyrics, not just good lyrics —

Some of the most impactful rock lyrics in the history of the form.

And then — and this is where I need your complete and undivided attention —
He never did it again.

Not close. Not approaching it. Not even attempting the same register.

Let's take You Shook Me All Night Long as an example. The song is a masterpiece. It is heard on radio stations around the world every single day. If Brian Johnson wrote that song — if that celebrations was his, if that instinct for language was his, if that wit was his —

Where did it go?

There is no follow up to that song. There is no "story of conquest song", with anything approaching that level of storytelling in AC/DC's subsequent catalogue. Why?

A man does not write a blockbuster hit like You Shook Me All Night Long and then spend his next twenty creative years not even trying to find the same frequency.

Exhibit Five. The Fingerprint. After.

Now let us look at what Brian Johnson demonstrably wrote once the album was finished and the world moved on.

The confirmed Johnson compositions from the albums that followed. For Those About to Rock. Flick of the Switch. Fly on the Wall. Album after album.

Look for the fingerprint.

It is gone.

The inhabited narrator — absent. In its place, the generic rock voice. The I that belongs to no one in particular and everyone in general.

The storytelling - not there at all. There is practically no vivid storytelling in any of AC/DC's subsequent records. 

The wit — gone. The double meaning that Bon Scott wove into everything he wrote, that architecture of two simultaneous levels — nowhere. What remains is direct. Unambiguous. One dimensional.

The mischief — gone. The warmth behind the transgression, the impish delight, the sense of a man who loves the world he is describing — replaced by something harder, colder, more mechanical.

And the swagger — and this is the most telling absence of all — the swagger becomes performance. It becomes the generic posture of a rock band rather than the specific confidence of one particular man who has earned every word he is singing.

This is not an attack on Brian Johnson. He is one of the greatest rock vocalists who ever lived. His instrument is extraordinary. His courage and his contribution are real.

But the fingerprint is gone. Completely. Consistently. From the moment Back in Black ended.
Which leaves us with two possibilities.

Either Brian Johnson discovered a literary genius of the most specific and extraordinary kind, deployed it once, on one album, in the weeks after joining a band — and then lost it so completely that not a trace of it ever appeared again in twenty years of subsequent work.

Or it was never his.

Exhibit Six. The Fingerprint at the Scene.

And now. Now that you know what you are looking at. Now that the fingerprint is burned into your understanding. Now that you have seen it blazing on every confirmed Bon Scott composition and found it entirely absent from everything Brian Johnson wrote before and everything he wrote after —
Now we open Back in Black.

I'm rolling thunder, pouring rain. I'm comin' on like a hurricane.

The narrator. Specific. Inhabited. Present. First person. 

The swagger. Unambiguous. Immediate. The opening lines of the album and already we know exactly whose voice is behind the voice.

Let me put my love into you. 

The mischief. The lightness behind the weight. Even here — even on the album made in the shadow of death — there is a grin.

Exhibit Five. The Fingerprint. After.

I said we would look at AC/DC before and after. We have seen before. Now let us see after.

In the decades following *Back in Black*, AC/DC made many records. We have confirmed Johnson compositions from every album.

Look for the fingerprint.

It is gone.

The confessional first person — absent. The specific narrator with the specific life — gone. The storytelling - gone. The wit beneath the surface, the intelligence behind the swagger, the sense of one man's biography shaping every line — nowhere.

What remains is professional and committed and utterly without the quality we identified in Exhibit Two.

This is not an insult to AC/DC or Brian Johnson. They are unquestionably on of the greatest rock bands in history. Johnson is one of the great rock vocalists of his generation.

But the fingerprint is gone. And it was gone from the moment *Back in Black* ended.

Which means one of two things.

Either Brian Johnson found a literary voice of towering and specific genius, used it once on one album, and then permanently and inexplicably lost it.

Or it was never his voice to begin with.

**Exhibit Six. The Fingerprint at the Scene of the Crime.**

And now. Now that you know what to look for. Now that the fingerprint is burned into your understanding. Now that you have seen it on everything Bon Scott touched and found it absent from everything Brian Johnson demonstrably wrote alone —

Now we look at *Back in Black.*

*"I'm rolling thunder, pouring rain. I'm comin' on like a hurricane."*

The fingerprint.

*"Takin' more than her share, had me fightin' for air
She told me to come, but I was already there"*

The fingerprint. The wit the mischief.

*"Forget the hearse 'cause I never die."

"I'm Back in Black, Yes I'm back in black."*

The fingerprint. The swagger. A declaration in first person. From a *particular* person. To a world that knew him. A return. An announcement of survival.

And now. Right now. With everything you know.

*"I've got nine lives — cat's eyes."*

Members of the jury.

In 1974, Bon Scott was involved in a near-fatal motorcycle accident. He was not expected to survive. He did survive. And he told people close to him that he had used up one of his nine lives.

That is not a lyric.

That is a *memory.*

A private memory. Belonging to one man. About one night. Written in the first person. On the best-selling rock album in history. By a man who — we are told — had never met Bon Scott. Had no nine lives story. Had no connection to that night or that accident or that private mythology.

There is only one explanation that does not require you to believe in a miracle of coincidence.

Bon Scott wrote that line.

Bon Scott wrote those words before he died. Perhaps they were Notes, fragments, verses — the raw materials of what would become *Back in Black.* And the band, in their grief and their love and their refusal to let him disappear, built an album around what he left behind.

Brian Johnson sang those words. He sang them magnificently — with fire and power and his own extraordinary instrument. His contribution was real.

But the *words* were not his.

The fingerprint proves it.

**Setting the Record Straight.**

The story the world knows is a good story. The hero. Stepping into the impossible. Delivering beyond all expectation.

I am not here to diminish that story entirely.

Brian Johnson walked into one of the hardest situations in rock history and he did not flinch. That is real. That deserves recognition.

But Bon Scott also deserves recognition. Bon Scott deserves the truth.

A man who spent his entire life writing himself into his music — specifically, personally, without disguise or distance — deserves to have his final words attributed to him.

The fingerprint is on *Back in Black.* It is pressed into every line. It is the same fingerprint we found on every confirmed Bon Scott composition and absent from every confirmed Brian Johnson composition before and after.

A fingerprint at a scene does not require malice.

In this case there was no malice. There was grief, and love, and a determination to finish what a great man had started.

But a fingerprint does establish presence.

Bon Scott was present on that album.

His words are there. His voice is there — not the voice you hear through the speakers, but the voice *behind* the voice. The literary voice. The confessional, specific, inhabited first person singular that belonged to him and to no one else.

All we are asking — all history requires — is that the record reflect what the evidence makes plain.

*Back in Black* was Bon Scott's final statement.

He wrote it.

He deserves his name on it.

The prosecution rests.

# **DID BON SCOTT KNOW HE WAS GOING TO DIE?**

---

*(AC/DC playing in the background. Highway to Hell. Someone sits down.)*

So you like this song?

Yeah everybody likes this song. 

But here's the thing.

If you really *listen* to it. Like really listen. And if you do — if you actually stop and pay attention to what's happening in that recording — there's something going on that I never heard anyone talk about.

Something that once I tell you, you're not gonna be able to unhear.

You want to hear it?

If you listen to early AC/DC, you'll hear that Bon Scott's voice is like a coiled rattlesnake. The guy sounds like he's about to fight everyone in the room simultaneously and he's genuinely looking forward to it. It's one of the most electric sounds in rock history. Genuinely.

Okay now listen to Highway to Hell.

Same guy. Three years later.

It's still incredible — but something has shifted. There's this looseness. This warmth. Like he's floating downstream instead of swimming upstream. Especially on the title track — the way he delivers "I'm on the highway to hell" isn't aggressive at all.

Like a man who's made a decision and he's completely okay with it.

Now here's the thing. That specific quality — that liquid, relaxed, leaning-back-into-it feeling — where does that come from? It almost sounds inebriated. But.. That's not what alcohol does to a voice. Alcohol makes you sloppy. Alcohol makes you fall off your stool.

This is something else. And people who know about these things would recognise it immediately.

And then there are the lyrics.

*"No stop signs, speed limit — nobody gonna slow me down."*

*"Hey Satan, paying my dues."*

*"I'm on the highway to hell."*

He recorded that eight months before he died.

Eight months.

Okay so now I need to take you somewhere for a second because this is where it gets interesting.

You know the Robert Johnson story?

Robert Johnson. Delta blues. 1930s Mississippi. By most accounts the greatest blues guitarist who ever lived — so far ahead of everyone around him that people genuinely didn't know what to make of him. And the legend — the story everyone tells — is that he went down to the crossroads at midnight and sold his soul to the devil in exchange for his gift.

What the Robert Johnson story is really describing is the bargain. The artistic bargain. You give me access to something transcendent — something beyond what normal people can reach — and I'll pay whatever the price is.

Robert Johnson died at 27. Cause of death unknown. Probably poisoned but nobody really investigated.

27.

You know who else died at 27? Hendrix. Joplin. Jim Morrison. Kurt Cobain. Amy Winehouse.

The 27 Club isn't a coincidence man. It's what the bargain looks like when it comes due.

Bon Scott made it to 33 which honestly — given what we're about to talk about — is kind of remarkable.

These weren't stupid people making stupid decisions. These were some of the most gifted artists of the twentieth century making a very specific calculated bet.

The bet goes like this. This thing gives me access to something I can't get any other way. And yeah it's probably gonna kill me. And I'm doing it anyway. Because what it produces is worth more to me than whatever years it costs.

Devil's bargain. Always has been this way.

But it's a real bargain that real artists have made over and over throughout history.

And I think Bon Scott made it.

Now here's the thing about Bon that most people don't know.

The image — sweaty shirtless larrikin, working class guy from the wrong side of town, all swagger and beer — that was completely real. He grew up in Fremantle which is a rough port town in Western Australia. Left school young. Scrapes with the law. He wasn't performing working class. He was the genuine article.

But the guy also wrote poetry... OK mostly limericks, but he had this whole interior life that his public image never came close to capturing. People who actually knew him describe this curiosity, this genuine intellectual engagement with the world that you'd never guess from watching him onstage.

Think about what's happening in 1979. Punk on one side. Disco on the other.

Punk is nihilist. No future. Burn it all down. The Pistols weren't trying to be a great rock band — they were trying to blow up the idea of rock bands. And behind all that working class rage? Mostly art school kids. The Clash were art school. Siouxsie was art school. Even the Pistols were essentially a Malcolm McLaren art project.

Disco meanwhile is Studio 54 and glamour and sophistication and everything that felt alien to a certain kind of working class kid in the American midwest and south.

And AC/DC are standing there going — guys. Guys. It's just rock and roll. It's a guitar and a riff and Angus running around like a lunatic who can't believe how good this feels. Isn't this the greatest thing you've ever seen?

That's the key thing about them. They weren't against anything. They were for everything. For rock and roll. For a good time. For the pure visceral joy of being alive and turned up to eleven.

Which is exactly why they connected with that specific working class American audience the way they did. Post Vietnam, post Watergate, Carter talking about national malaise — the country felt defeated. And here come these five Australian lunatics offering something completely different. Not escape into glamour. Not escape into nihilism.

Just — come on. Feel this. Right now. Isn't it great to be alive.

For a very specific kind of American kid that was like oxygen.

Now. Put all of that together and then imagine what happens when Bon Scott dies in February 1980 and the real story comes out.

Not alcohol. Something else.

First — American radio. FM rock radio in 1980 was the absolute gatekeeper of everything and program directors answered to advertisers who didn't want controversy anywhere near their Chevrolet commercials. It didn't need a formal ban. It just needed fifty guys in fifty cities independently going "yeah I'm not touching that." And suddenly you don't exist.

But the deeper problem is the cultural thing.

You remember Disco Demolition Night?

July 1979. Comiskey Park Chicago. A DJ named Steve Dahl blows up a crate of disco records in the outfield and fifty thousand people storm the field and it turns into an actual riot. They cancel the second game of the doubleheader. It's on the news.

That's how the AC/DC audience felt about the other side of the culture war.

And heroin in 1980 was associated with exactly that world. Downtown Manhattan. Studio 54. Andy Warhol. Lou Reed. The Velvet Underground. The whole art school bohemian scene.

It was the enemy's drug.

So a heroin connection wouldn't just have killed AC/DC on radio. It would've felt like a betrayal. Like finding out your guy was secretly on the other side the whole time.

And here's the really tragic part. Heroin is a withdrawal drug. A disappearing drug. It's for people who need the world to go away for a while.

And AC/DC were the most emphatically present band in rock history. Everything about them was here, now, all the way turned up.

So the idea of Bon using it isn't just commercially catastrophic. It's heartbreaking. Because it means that underneath all that joyful noise — the guy who made everyone feel completely alive — was sometimes a man who needed the world to go quiet.

Think about Jim Morrison. Found dead in a bathtub in Paris in 1971. No autopsy. Cause of death — heart failure. Case closed in about 48 hours. The Doors' back catalogue has been selling ever since.

No conspiracy required. Just a machine that needed to keep moving.

And AC/DC's machine was about to become the biggest in rock history.

Back in Black — the album they made five months after Bon died — has sold fifty million copies. Fifty. Million. Second biggest selling album in the history of recorded music. You know what's ahead of it? One thing. Thriller. That's it.

The money from that album and the career it launched runs into hundreds of millions of dollars. Still coming in today. Every movie trailer. Every stadium show. Every wedding DJ.

Did anyone conspire? Did anyone sit in a room and get their stories straight?

Almost certainly not. That's not how it works. It never works like that.

What happens is simpler and somehow worse. Nobody asks the question they don't want answered. The coroner does the minimum. The people in the room let the simplest story stand because the alternative is a disaster nobody wants to deal with.

And by the way — the full toxicology results from Bon Scott's inquest were never made public.

Never. Made. Public.

For a death officially caused by alcohol — something provable with a blood test — we've never seen the complete picture of what was in his system that night.

Forty five years later.

We still don't know.

And the night he died — he was out with a friend, drinking heavily, friend drives him home, can't get him inside, leaves him in the car in February in London, goes inside, goes to sleep.

Doesn't check on him until the following afternoon.

The following afternoon.

Come on man.

We're not saying the guy did anything wrong. We believe his account completely. Terrible tragic call. People make awful decisions in awful moments.

But that's not the behaviour of someone who thinks their friend had too much to drink. That's someone who's scared. Someone for whom the situation feels too complicated to look at in the light of day.

So here's where we land.

Bon Scott was a genuine artist. Smarter and more self aware than anyone ever gave him credit for. A working class kid with a bohemian soul who found something in his final period — a state, a depth, a creative place he hadn't been able to reach before — and made the same bargain that Robert Johnson made at the crossroads. That Charlie Parker made every single day of his adult life.

The work's worth it.

Even if it kills you.

And I think somewhere in that beautiful dangerous place he'd found — with the London night scene swirling around him and the music coming out unlike anything he'd ever made before — he knew.

He put it right there in the song.

*"I'm on the highway to hell."*

He wasn't being metaphorical.

He was telling us exactly where he was going.

We just weren't paying attention.

Go listen to Highway to Hell tonight. Really listen. From the beginning. All the way through.

You'll hear it.

*(Let it play. All the way out. Don't fade it.)*

*(Black.)*

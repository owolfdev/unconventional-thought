# The Films Tarantino Should Have Made After Jackie Brown

---

## HOOK

I want to talk about one of the greatest bodies of work in modern American cinema. A body of work that's lost to history. Never to be recovered. The lost works of Quentin Tarantino.

Tarantino came out of the gate like a 10,000 volt electric shock — Reservoir Dogs, Pulp Fiction, Jackie Brown, and somehow True Romance in the same run. That is an absurd and glorious opening stretch. We're talking Scorsese. Leone. Lumet. That conversation. That company.

And then he went and made the shelf.

This is NOT a takedown. Tarantino is a genius. I'm one of his biggest fans. But I want to talk about what Jackie Brown was pointing toward — the body of work that was right there, that we can almost see the shape of — and what we got instead.

---

## THE RUN

Quick history. Stay with me.

1992. Reservoir Dogs. Cannot be overstated. It was a love letter to everyone who traded VHS tapes and mixtapes — Quentin was that guy, and he hit it out of the park. Like when the Ramones came out. Everyone wanted to be a filmmaker overnight.

Six men, a warehouse, a heist already gone wrong before the story starts. Dialogue pouring out of these characters like you'd just walked into a conversation that had been going on for years. Nobody had heard American men talk like that on screen. Violent and funny and completely alive.

And the technique — extraordinary from film one. Take the ear-cutting scene. Tarantino does something almost nobody does — he cuts away. You don't see it. You see Mr. Blonde dancing to "Stuck in the Middle with You." The music in this film is insane, by the way — like someone raided a thrift store bin and found gold. But back to the ear. You hear the screaming. Your imagination does the rest. That is not a young filmmaker showing off. That is genuine control from a first film.

1993. True Romance. He writes it, Tony Scott directs it. More commercial, more kinetic. But the script is so alive, so full of genuine romantic feeling under all the carnage, it survives the translation. And it showed something important — Tarantino could write a love story. Real love. Messy, irrational, dangerous love between imperfect people. File that away.

1994. Pulp Fiction. What do you say. Bases-loaded grand slam. Royale with Cheese. The dance. The briefcase. Jules and Vincent in the apartment. Butch going back for the watch. Non-linear structure used not as a gimmick but as a moral statement — the film is about redemption and it withholds information the same way grace does. You don't realize what you're watching until you've seen it twice. That is not an accident.

Palme d'Or at Cannes. Two hundred million on an eight million budget. Single-handedly resurrects John Travolta's career — which in retrospect may not have been an unqualified gift to the world, but still. Impressive.

And then. Jackie Brown. 1997.

Pay attention here. Because Jackie Brown isn't just the best film in this run. It's the most important film in this whole story. Not because of what it is. Because of where it was pointing.

---

## WHAT JACKIE BROWN ACTUALLY IS

Jackie Brown is a film of pure craft.

Not "wouldn't it be cool if." Not a concept. Not a genre exercise. Not a mixtape of influences arranged into something slick and referential. People. Place. Consequence.

Pam Grier. Robert Forster. Samuel L. Jackson. Robert De Niro doing a masterclass in deliberate subtlety. A middle-aged Black woman navigating two dangerous men using nothing but her intelligence and her dignity. No superhero moments. No cool speeches. Just a woman who has been underestimated her whole life deciding this time she's going to win. Yes, it's borrowed from Elmore Leonard — but like pulling Travolta out of the bargain bin at Blockbuster, Tarantino puts his own brilliant spin on it.

The film is patient in a way none of his others are. The scene where Robert Forster and Pam Grier sit in his car and listen to the Delfonics — nothing happens. Everything happens. That is one of the most romantic scenes in American cinema and there is not a single kiss in it.

And it is grounded in a specific Los Angeles. Not a stylized LA. Not a movie LA. The LA of strip malls and bail bondsmen and mid-level criminals who've been in this city long enough to know exactly what it costs. The geography is a character. The light is a character. That specific listlessness — the way LA curdles your ambitions into something resigned and cynical — it's in every single frame.

You don't need a Japanese castle. You don't need Nazi-occupied France. You need a place you know completely and the discipline to stay there.

When I saw Jackie Brown I was floored. I was waiting for what came next like you wait for the next episode of a show you can't leave. And what did audiences say? "Yeah it's good... but where's the cool stuff?" "It's a bit slow." "It's not really HIM." Not Pulp Fiction money. Not Pulp Fiction reviews. And Tarantino — who is human, who reads his press — heard the message.

Give them the cool stuff.

Here's the thing. Tarantino is one of the most cinematically literate people alive. He could give a three-hour lecture on exactly why Jackie Brown was pointing somewhere more profound than anything that came after. He knows. He has always known.

And he listened anyway. Because ten million people saying "give us the cool stuff" is very hard to resist.

So what did we get?

Kill Bill. The minute I saw that poster I knew it was over. Uma Thurman. Yellow tracksuit. Samurai sword. I went anyway. I tried. But come on — that was a Michelin-starred mess. The emperor with no clothes.

Kill Bill 2 is actually better. Quieter. More patient. The Pai Mei scenes have texture. Bill's death is almost moving — almost — but we still don't know these people. We were handed a mission and a color palette instead of characters and a world. When Beatrix wins, you feel satisfied the way you feel satisfied finishing a bag of crisps. Full. Empty. Already forgotten.

Death Proof. Tarantino's love letter to car stunts. There are conversations in that film — the girls in the diner, the road trip — that feel almost like the early Tarantino. People talking in a real place. And then Kurt Russell shows up and it becomes a stunt reel with dialogue stapled on top. A ghost of the film it could have been. A very fast ghost.

Inglourious Basterds — OK, I'll be fair, because this is the one you'll come for me on. Christoph Waltz is extraordinary. The basement bar scene is a masterclass in tension. Technically? Magnificent. But here's the thing: Inglourious Basterds is a film about cinema, not about people. The propaganda, the projection booth, film as the weapon of revenge — Tarantino thinking about movies instead of thinking about life. Fine as a one-off. Not fine as the logical successor to Jackie Brown.

Django Unchained genuinely hurts because it has something Jackie Brown had — a real subject. Slavery. Dignity. A Black man navigating a white world that wants to erase him. Devastating material. Buried under two and a half hours of spaghetti western pastiche and a DiCaprio performance so enjoyably deranged you forget you were supposed to feel anything. Someone wrapped a real wound in tinfoil and fireworks. Dazzling. Gone in two days.

The Hateful Eight. Eight people, one room — this is the premise that should have delivered. And it's three hours of people being spectacularly horrible to each other with zero emotional payoff, because none of them are people you know. They are functions in a mystery. Magnificently dressed functions. Gorgeous cinematography. Technical marvel. You leave cold as a January sidewalk.

Once Upon a Time in Hollywood. Here is the wild one. Because this is actually the best film in his post-Jackie Brown run — and that is somehow the most damning thing I can say about the rest of them.

It's patient. It's located. Soaked in a specific Los Angeles at a specific moment. DiCaprio and Pitt are wonderful together — genuinely. There are scenes that almost feel like Jackie Brown. You lean forward. You think: he's coming home. After all this time. And then, with twenty minutes to go, he winks at the camera. Cool ending. Cathartic rewrite of history. The "wouldn't it be cool if" he simply could not resist. After two hours of almost being a real film, he reminds you it was always just a movie. That is not a stumble — that is a choice. And it's the peak of everything he made after Jackie Brown. The best of the shelf. Which tells you everything you need to know about the shelf.

## THE TECHNIQUES THAT HAD MORE TO GIVE

Jackie Brown wasn't just a good film. It was a proof of concept. Techniques just getting started that in a different timeline could have built something genuinely extraordinary.

**One place.** Leone never left the West. Scorsese never really left New York. The Coens never stopped being midwesterners. And those are the bodies of work we return to. Because constraint produces depth. And depth is the only thing that lasts.

Here's the dirty secret about cool — cool has a shelf life. Uma Thurman in a yellow tracksuit was cool in 2003. Robert Forster and Pam Grier in a parking lot listening to the Delfonics is not cool. It's true. And true does not age.

Choosing one place is a statement of artistic integrity. I am going to know this world completely. I am not going to jump to feudal Japan when I run out of ideas. I am going to stay here — in this city, with these people — and find everything I need right here. Because it's all here. It was always all here. When you can't hide behind a Japanese castle, you have to make a strip mall in Compton feel as loaded as the Colosseum. That is the hardest thing in cinema. It's also the thing that separates films people watch twice from films people watch once and think were cool.

**Nonlinear time — across films.** Pulp Fiction scrambled time inside one film as a moral statement — the structure was the argument. But that technique had barely started. Because scrambling time inside one film is one thing. Scrambling time across multiple films is something else entirely. Imagine watching film three and suddenly understanding something from film one differently. A moment you thought you understood — suddenly carrying the weight of two more films of context. You wouldn't just be watching a new film. You'd be re-watching every film that came before it. Through new eyes. Every time.

**Character development that compounds.** After Jackie Brown — do you genuinely care about any of Tarantino's characters? Not enjoy. Care. The Bride in Kill Bill is impressive. Hans Landa is a magnificent creation. But do you grieve them? Jackie Brown and Max Cherry not ending up together costs you something real. Mr. Orange bleeding out costs you something real. Because those characters lived in a world with consistent rules and consequences. The Bride is a function. She exists to complete a mission. She arrives fully formed and leaves fully formed. You don't carry her with you. Jackie Brown changes. Slowly. Credibly. Through accumulated pressure of real circumstances. That is what was just getting started.

**Recurring characters as emotional architecture.** He was doing this instinctively. Vic Vega and Vincent Vega are brothers. Red Apple cigarettes. Big Kahuna Burger. The same Los Angeles bleeding through. He was building connective tissue almost by accident — even planned Double V Vega, putting both brothers on screen before either film's events. Never got made. But the instinct was right. A recurring character across multiple films in a consistent world gives you somewhere to put your feelings. Someone whose presence in a new scene lands differently because of everything you already know about them. Not a franchise. Not a superhero universe. The way a face you recognize in a city you know changes how you see the whole city.

**Restraint as the highest power.** He cuts away from the ear. The Delfonics scene — two people, a car, nothing happens, everything happens. The shopping mall in Jackie Brown, the same location from three perspectives, no action, just information landing at different weights. Tarantino at his best withholds. Gives you less than you expect, makes you feel more than you thought possible. And then — Kill Bill. Inglourious Basterds. Django. More. Bigger. Louder. Each film giving you everything immediately. Each film trusting the audience less. And each one, despite being entertaining, landing a little lighter than the one before. Because restraint compounds. Every time you withhold something and the audience feels it anyway, you're teaching them to trust you. That trust takes years to build and one franchise to spend.

---

## WHAT WE LOST — AND WHAT HE SHOULD HAVE MADE INSTEAD

Here is the thing about Kill Bill, Django, Inglourious Basterds, The Hateful Eight, Once Upon a Time. Every single one is a film that would look great on the shelf of a video store. Specifically the video store where one of Tarantino's real characters works.

Every post-Jackie Brown film is something a Tarantino character would recommend from behind a counter. "Oh man, you haven't seen this? It's this insane alternate history thing where Jewish soldiers scalp Nazis and it ends in a cinema and—" Yes. Great recommendation. Put it on the shelf. Rent it Friday night.

But what is the film that video store clerk is actually living? What does the strip mall look like at 11pm when he locks up? What does LA cost him? THAT is the film Tarantino should have made. That IS Jackie Brown. And he walked away from it and made the shelf instead.

What if — after Jackie Brown — he'd just said: I live in Los Angeles. I know this city. I know what it smells like at 2am. I'm going to make four films set here, different characters, different stories, same moral universe. And Kill Bill, Django, Inglourious Basterds — those are the films the characters in my real films watch on their days off. They exist. They're great. They live on the shelf. But I'm not going to make them. I have something more important to make.

That's what artistic integrity looks like. Not being solemn and self-important — knowing the difference between the film you want to make and the film you are built to make. Scorsese has stayed in his room for fifty years. Leone never left the West. And those bodies of work compound. Every new entry recontextualizes everything before it.

Jackie Brown was where the clerk became the artist. Where the obsession stopped being the point and started being the tool. And then the audience asked for the obsession back. Of course he gave it to them. The obsession is more fun to make. The obsession gets standing ovations at Cannes. The obsession doesn't require you to spend three years making a strip mall in Compton feel like the most important place on earth.

Instead we got the shelf. A very impressive shelf. Great films to recommend to people.

Reservoir Dogs. True Romance. Pulp Fiction. Jackie Brown.

One of the greatest creative runs in the history of American cinema — pointing clearly, unmistakably toward something even greater. And somewhere between the Delfonics in a parking lot in Compton and O-Ren Ishii's anime backstory, we lost the thread.

Tell me in the comments if I'm wrong. And before you come at me with your Kill Bill stans and your Basterds defenders — great films, love them, they look beautiful on the shelf — it doesn't change what Jackie Brown was pointing toward. It doesn't give us back the LA we were just beginning to know.

Thank you. Goodbye.

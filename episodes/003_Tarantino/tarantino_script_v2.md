## SECTION 1 — THE RUN

[matter-of-fact] OK. Quick history. Stay with me because this matters.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] 1992. Reservoir Dogs. I think it cant be overstated what a revelation reservoir dogs was. It was a homage to everyone who love cinema and music at the time. It was for people who traded VHS tapes of the the best films and mix tapes of the best music. Quentin was one of those people. And he it a home run. It was like when the Ramones came out. Everyone wanted to be a filmmaker. 

Six men, a warehouse, a heist that already went wrong before the story starts. Dialogue pouring out of these characters like they'd been talking their whole lives and you'd just walked in mid-conversation. [pause] Nobody had heard American men talk like that on screen. [quickly] Violent and funny and completely alive.

[matter-of-fact] The technique — [pause] already extraordinary on the first film. The violence... Tarantino took you there. Close up and sustained violence. Breathtaking. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Take the ear cutting scene. Tarantino does something almost nobody does — he cuts away. [pause] You don't see it. You see Mr Blonde dancing, to that song! Omg the music in this film was unreal. It's like someone raided a thift store music bin and just found gold. But I digress. Back to the ear cutting scene. You hear the screaming. [pause] Your imagination does the rest. [quickly][matter-of-fact] That is not a young filmmaker showing off. That is control. [slowly] Genuine artistic control. From a first film.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] 1993. True Romance. He writes it, Tony Scott directs it. Different sensibility entirely — more commercial, more kinetic. But something extraordinary happens anyway. [pause] Because the script is so alive, so full of genuine romantic feeling underneath all the carnage — [quickly] it survives the translation. [pause] And it showed something important. Tarantino could write a love story. [pause] Real love. Messy, dangerous, irrational love between imperfect people. [slowly] File that away.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] 1994. Pulp Fiction. [pause] What do you say. If Reservoir Dogs was a home run, Pulp Fiction was a bases loaded, out of the park, grand slam home run. What!? [matter-of-fact] Non-linear structure used not as a gimmick but as a statement. The film is literally about redemption and it withholds and reveals information the same way grace does. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Pulp Fiction wins the Palme d'Or at Cannes. Two hundred million dollars on an eight million dollar budget. Single-handedly resurrects John Travolta's career. [sarcastic] Which in retrospect may not have been an unqualified gift to the world but still. [matter-of-fact] Impressive.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] And here is where I need you to pay attention. Because Jackie Brown may not just be the best film in this run. [pause] It is the most important film in this entire story. [slowly] Not because of what it is. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Because of where it was pointing.

## SECTION 2 — WHAT JACKIE BROWN ACTUALLY IS

[matter-of-fact] Jackie Brown is a film of pure craft.

[pause]

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Not — [slowly] "wouldn't it be cool if." [quickly][matter-of-fact] Not a concept. Not a genre exercise. Not a mixtape of influences arranged into something slick and referential. No it's all about People. Place. Consequence. [pause] Pure craft, serving the story, on every level. 

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Pam Grier. Robert Forster. Samuel L Jackson. Robert De Niro in a performance of magnificent deliberate subtlety. [pause] A middle-aged Black woman navigating the gap between two dangerous men using nothing but her intelligence and her dignity. [quickly][matter-of-fact] No superhero moments. No cool speeches. [pause] Just a woman who has been underestimated her entire life deciding that this time — [pause] this one last time — [slowly] she is going to win. That's a story. Yes it's borrowed material, from an Elmore Leonard novel. But just like pulling Travolta out of the bargin bin at Blockbuster, Tarantino puts his own brilliant spin on the story. 

[quickly][matter-of-fact] The film is patient in a way none of his others are. There are scenes that just breathe. [pause] The scene where Robert Forster and Pam Grier listen to the Delfonics in his car. [pause] Nothing happens. [pause] Everything happens. [quickly][matter-of-fact] That is one of the most romantic scenes in American cinema and there is not a single kiss in it. [pause] That is a filmmaker at total command of his instrument.

[matter-of-fact] It is grounded in a specific place. [pause] Los Angeles. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Not a stylized LA. Not a movie LA. [pause] The LA of strip malls and bail bondsmen and mid-level criminals and people who have been in this city long enough to understand exactly what it costs. [quickly][matter-of-fact] The geography is a character. The light is a character. The specific listlessness of that city — the way LA curdles people's ambitions into something resigned and cynical... [pause] that is in every frame of the film.

[slowly][deliberately] That is what pure craft looks like. [quickly][matter-of-fact] You don't need a Japanese castle. You don't need Nazi-occupied France. You don't need a concept that can be pitched in one sentence in an elevator like a sweaty wiz kid overdosed on caffeine and sugar. [pause] [deliberately] You need a place you know completely. Characters who live in it fully. [slowly] And the discipline to stay there.

When I first say Jackie Brown I was floored by how mature it was, how restrained, how emotional and effective. I was anticipating Tarantino's next films with excitement and optimism. Like when you are waiting for the next episode of your favorite Netflix drama to drop. You don't want to leave that world. You want more. [quickly][sarcastic] So, how did general audiences react to Jackie Brown? What did they say?  [pause] "Yeah it's good... but where's the cool stuff?" [condescending] "It's a bit slow isn't it?" [matter-of-fact] "It's not really HIM." [quickly] Also the film made money, but not Pulp Fiction money. The reviews are good but not Pulp Fiction reviews. [pause] And Tarantino — who is human, who reads his press, who has ears — [pause] hears the message loud and clear.

[slowly][dramatically] Give them the cool stuff.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] Now here's the thing. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Tarantino is one of the most cinematically literate human beings who has ever lived. He has seen everything. He has absorbed everything. [pause] He could give a three-hour lecture on exactly why Jackie Brown represented a more sustainable and more profound artistic direction than anything that came after it. [slowly] He knows. [pause] He has always known.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] And he listened anyway. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Because he is human. Because the noise was loud. Because "give us the cool stuff" is an incredibly hard thing to resist when ten million people are saying it simultaneously.

[pause]

So what did we get? We got what I call the "wouldn't it be cool if" era. Pretty much the rest of his career. Ugh. 

We got Kill Bill. Oh god. The minute I saw the first Kill Bill poster, I knew it was over. What a gut punch. Uma Thurman? Love her but... Uma Thurman as a bad-ass killer ninja with a samurai sword and a yellow Bruce Lee track suit? Yea right... At least cast a real bad-ass if you are going to make a bad-ass movie. Isn't Tarantino supposed to be a casting genius? But I digress. Well... so, I went and saw Kill Bill anyway. I tried to like it. But come on guys... Let's be honest. It was a mess. Excuse my French, but that was a highly polished, michelin starred, shit sandwich. A face plant, the emperor with no clothes. Jesus, I sound bitter!

## SECTION 3 — THE TECHNIQUES THAT HAD MORE TO GIVE

[matter-of-fact] So let's talk about craft. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Because Jackie Brown wasn't just a good film. It was a proof of concept. A demonstration of techniques that were just getting started. [pause] Techniques that — in a different timeline — could have built something genuinely extraordinary.

[pause]


### TECHNIQUE 1: ONE PLACE

[matter-of-fact] Leone never left the West. Scorsese never really left New York. The Coens never really left the American heartland. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And it is not a coincidence that those are the bodies of work we return to. That those are the worlds we feel we actually inhabit. [pause] Because constraint produces depth.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] When you cannot wow people with a new location — when you cannot hide behind a Japanese castle or a World War Two chateau — [pause] you have to make a strip mall in Compton feel as loaded as the Colosseum. [quickly][matter-of-fact] You have to find the drama inside the ordinary. The violence inside the mundane. The romance inside a car in a parking lot. [slowly] That is the hardest thing in cinema. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And it is the thing Jackie Brown proved Tarantino could do.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] Imagine four films. All set in LA. All in this world. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Not the same characters necessarily. Not a franchise. [pause] But the same city. The same moral atmosphere. The same understanding of what things cost here. [quickly][matter-of-fact] By the time you reach the fourth film you would not just be watching a movie. [slowly][dramatically] You would be going home.

[pause]

### TECHNIQUE 2: NONLINEAR TIME — ACROSS FILMS

[matter-of-fact] Pulp Fiction scrambled time inside one film. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And the effect was extraordinary — not as a gimmick but as a moral statement. The structure was the argument. [pause] Withholding and revealing information the way grace does. [quickly][matter-of-fact] You don't realize what you're watching until you've seen it twice.

[matter-of-fact] But here is what nobody talks about. [quickly][matter-of-fact] That technique was not finished. Not even close. [pause] It had barely started. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Because scrambling time inside one film is one thing. [pause] Scrambling time across multiple films — [slowly] that is something else entirely.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Imagine watching film three and suddenly understanding something from film one differently. [pause] A scene you thought you understood — a conversation, a decision, a moment — [quickly][matter-of-fact] suddenly carrying the weight of two more films of context. [pause] The rewatch value alone would be extraordinary. [quickly][matter-of-fact] You would not just be watching a new film. You would be re-watching every film that came before it simultaneously. [slowly][dramatically] Through new eyes. Every time.

[pause]

### TECHNIQUE 3: CHARACTER DEVELOPMENT THAT COMPOUNDS

[matter-of-fact] Here is a question. [quickly][matter-of-fact] After Jackie Brown — do you genuinely care about any of Tarantino's characters? [pause] Not enjoy. [slowly] Care. [quickly][matter-of-fact] The Bride in Kill Bill is impressive. Hans Landa is a magnificent creation. [pause] But do you grieve them? Do you carry them with you?

[matter-of-fact] Jackie Brown and Max Cherry not ending up together costs you something real. [pause] Mr Orange bleeding out on that warehouse floor costs you something real. [pause] Vincent Vega dying on that bathroom floor — [slowly] you feel that. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Because those characters lived in a world with consistent rules and consistent consequences and you understood what things meant there.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Kill Bill's Bride is a function. She exists to complete a mission. [pause] You admire her. You root for her. [quickly][matter-of-fact] But she does not develop. She does not change in ways that feel earned through living. [pause] She arrives fully formed and leaves fully formed and the film is thrilling and [sarcastic] yes fine very impressive — [matter-of-fact] but you do not carry her with you.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Jackie Brown changes. Slowly. Credibly. Through the accumulated pressure of real circumstances and real decisions. [pause] Max Cherry changes. [quickly][matter-of-fact] You watch it happen in real time and you feel it because it is earned. [slowly][dramatically] That is the technique. That is what was just getting started.

[pause]

### TECHNIQUE 4: THE RECURRING CHARACTER AS EMOTIONAL ARCHITECTURE

[matter-of-fact] He was already doing this instinctively. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Vic Vega and Vincent Vega are brothers. The same universe bleeding through. Red Apple cigarettes. Big Kahuna Burger. The same Los Angeles appearing and reappearing. [pause] He was building connective tissue. Almost accidentally. [quickly][matter-of-fact] He even planned Double V Vega — putting both Vega brothers on screen together before the events of either film. [pause] It never got made.

[matter-of-fact] But the instinct was always there. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And the instinct was right. Because what a recurring character does — across multiple films, in a consistent world — [pause] is give you somewhere to put your feelings. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Someone whose history you know. Whose cost you understand. Whose presence in a new scene lands differently because of everything you already know about them.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Not a franchise. Not a superhero universe. [pause] Something quieter and more serious than that. [slowly][deliberately] The way a face you recognize in a city you know changes how you see the whole city.

[pause]

### TECHNIQUE 5: RESTRAINT AS THE HIGHEST POWER

[matter-of-fact] The ear cutting scene. [quickly][matter-of-fact] He cuts away. You don't see it. Your imagination does the rest. [pause] The Delfonics scene. Two people in a car. Nothing happens. [pause] The shopping mall scene in Jackie Brown — [quickly][matter-of-fact] the same location shown three times from three perspectives, each time revealing something new — [pause] no action, no set piece, just information landing at different weights.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Tarantino at his best withholds. [pause] Gives you less than you expect and makes you feel more than you thought possible. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And then — Kill Bill. Inglourious Basterds. Django. [pause] More. Bigger. Louder. More stylized. More referential. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Each film giving you everything immediately. Each film trusting the audience less to meet it halfway. [slowly][deliberately] And each film — despite being entertaining, despite being fun — landing a little lighter than the one before it.

[matter-of-fact] Because restraint compounds. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Every time you withhold something and the audience feels it anyway — you are teaching them to trust you. [pause] Teaching them that silence means something in your films. That a held moment costs something. [quickly][matter-of-fact] That trust is the most valuable thing a filmmaker can build with an audience. [slowly][dramatically] And it takes years to build and one franchise to spend.

## SECTION 4 — WHAT WE LOST

[matter-of-fact] So. Inglourious Basterds. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Jewish Nazi hunters. Alternate history. Extraordinary performances. Fun film. [pause] Genuinely fun film. [sarcastic] Great cinema. Very cinematic. [matter-of-fact] But I want to say something about it that I think is true.

[pause]

[slowly][conspiratorial] Inglourious Basterds is the kind of film that should exist as a VHS tape on the shelf of the video store where one of Tarantino's real characters works.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] Not a dismissal. [quickly][matter-of-fact] An observation about what kind of film it is. [pause] It is the film the video store clerk dreams about making. The brilliant, obsessive, cinematically-saturated kid who has seen everything and wants to make something from all of it. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Tarantino was that clerk. He was always that clerk. And his early films were extraordinary precisely because he brought that obsession into contact with real human material — [pause] real place, real people, real consequence — [quickly][matter-of-fact] and the collision produced something that transcended both.

[quickly][matter-of-fact] Jackie Brown was where the clerk became the artist. [pause] Where the obsession stopped being the point and started being the tool. [slowly][dramatically] And then the audience asked for the obsession back. [pause] And he gave it to them.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] The loss is not specific films. [quickly][matter-of-fact] It is a standard. A level of craft. A discipline of place and character and restraint and accumulated emotional weight — [pause] that those first four films were building toward. [quickly][matter-of-fact] A body of work that would have deepened with every entry. That would have given us characters we carried with us and a world we returned to and a filmmaker whose next film we needed the way you need the next chapter of a book you cannot put down.

[pause]

[slowly][dramatically] That is what we lost.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] Not because Tarantino lacked the vision. [quickly][matter-of-fact] Not because he didn't know. [pause] But because the signal was loud and he is human and "give us the cool stuff" is very hard to resist when the whole world is saying it.

[pause]

[slowly] Reservoir Dogs. True Romance. Pulp Fiction. Jackie Brown.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] One of the greatest creative runs in the history of American cinema. [quickly][matter-of-fact] A run that was pointing — clearly, unmistakably — toward something even greater. [pause] And somewhere between the Delfonics playing in a parking lot in Compton [pause] and O-Ren Ishii's anime backstory — [slowly][dramatically] we lost the thread.

[pause]

[matter-of-fact] Tell me in the comments if I'm wrong. [quickly][matter-of-fact] And before you come at me — yes Kill Bill is fun, yes Inglourious Basterds is brilliant in its own way, yes Once Upon a Time in Hollywood has its moments — [sarcastic] doesn't change what Jackie Brown was pointing toward. [pause] Doesn't make up for what we lost.

[slowly][dramatically] Thank you. Goodbye.
